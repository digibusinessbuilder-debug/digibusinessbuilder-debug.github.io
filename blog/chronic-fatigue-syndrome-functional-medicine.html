Addressing Chronic Fatigue Syndrome Through Functional Medicine

Jordan Casey, MS-CNFM · Master of Science in Clinical Nutrition and Functional Medicine, University of Western States

Medically reviewed by Fawad Ahmed, MD on October 13, 2025

Published October 13, 2025 · Updated October 15, 2026

Chronic Fatigue Syndrome (ME/CFS) goes far beyond everyday tiredness. Mitochondrial dysfunction, infections, toxins, and nutrient imbalances all play a role — and functional medicine can help address the root causes.

Book a Consultation

If you're living with unrelenting exhaustion, brain fog, dizziness, or "post-exertional crashes" that can last for days, you're not alone. Chronic Fatigue Syndrome (CFS), also called Myalgic Encephalomyelitis (ME), is a complex, multi-system condition that disrupts energy production, recovery, and stress tolerance.

While conventional medicine often focuses on symptom management, functional medicine looks deeper, addressing root causes such as mitochondrial dysfunction, environmental exposures, chronic infections, and nutrient deficiencies to help support more sustainable energy.

What is chronic fatigue syndrome?

CFS is a debilitating disorder characterized by persistent, unexplained fatigue lasting at least six months, along with a cluster of symptoms that affect multiple systems:

Common symptoms

  • Crushing fatigue that isn't relieved by sleep
  • Post-exertional malaise (PEM): symptom flare-ups after activity
  • Brain fog and cognitive difficulties
  • Dizziness or palpitations, often worse when standing
  • Muscle or joint pain
  • Non-restorative sleep
  • Digestive issues such as bloating or food sensitivities

Key statistics

CFS affects an estimated 3.3 million adults in the U.S. yet remains underdiagnosed and misunderstood. It is not "all in your head" — growing research shows clear physiological disruptions, particularly in energy metabolism and immune regulation.

The mitochondria–fatigue connection

Mitochondria are the energy powerhouses of our cells, producing ATP (adenosine triphosphate) to fuel everything from movement to hormone production. In people with CFS, mitochondrial energy production is often impaired.

Research has revealed reduced ATP output, altered oxygen utilization, and alterations in mitochondrial structure and gene expression. When your mitochondria can't produce energy efficiently, you feel it: physical activity leads to exaggerated fatigue, and recovery takes much longer.

Common causes of mitochondrial dysfunction in CFS

Functional medicine identifies several overlapping factors that can impair mitochondrial function:

Environmental Toxins

Mold exposure, heavy metals (like mercury or lead), pesticides, or solvents can damage mitochondrial enzymes and DNA. These low-level exposures often build up over time, subtly draining energy.

Chronic Infections

Viruses such as Epstein–Barr (EBV) or HHV-6, as well as mold colonization and Lyme disease, can reactivate or persist in tissues, interfering with mitochondrial function and triggering chronic inflammation.

Nutrient Imbalances

Key nutrients, including iron, copper, B vitamins, magnesium, CoQ10, and selenium, are essential for energy production. Deficiencies or imbalances can stall the 'energy assembly line,' leaving cells underpowered.

Gut–Immune Disruption

Leaky gut and dysbiosis can allow bacterial fragments (like LPS) to enter circulation, increasing inflammation and suppressing mitochondrial function. Many with CFS show altered microbiomes and increased intestinal permeability.

Functional medicine strategies for CFS

A functional medicine approach is personalized and multi-layered, targeting the root causes rather than just the symptoms. Common strategies include:

Reducing Toxic Load

Testing for and remediating mold, heavy metals, and chemical exposures; supporting detoxification with antioxidants and liver nutrients.

Treating Latent Infections

Using antiviral, antimicrobial, or immune-modulating protocols tailored to EBV, Lyme, or fungal colonization.

Mitochondrial Support

Supplementing B vitamins, magnesium, CoQ10, acetyl-L-carnitine, alpha-lipoic acid, and glutathione precursors to enhance oxidative phosphorylation.

Nutrient Repletion

Correcting iron, copper, and zinc imbalances to optimize oxygen delivery and mitochondrial enzyme function.

Supporting Gut Integrity

Addressing dysbiosis, SIBO/SIFO, and permeability to reduce systemic inflammation.

Lifestyle Interventions

Emphasizing pacing to avoid PEM, improving sleep hygiene, reducing psychological stressors, and implementing anti-inflammatory nutrition.

Personalized Treatment Approach

Each case is different, and every patient presents with unique triggers, mediators, and symptoms. A skilled functional medicine provider who specializes in CFS can help identify key areas that are foundational to address in each case.

When applied systematically, these interventions aim to support mitochondrial function, reduce the inflammatory burden, and rebuild resilience, with the goal of improving energy, cognition, and overall quality of life.

Practical steps for patients

For individuals living with CFS, recovery often involves incremental progress rather than quick fixes. Practical steps may include:

  • Identifying and remediating environmental exposures (e.g., testing for mold, using air filtration, reducing chemical load)
  • Testing for and correcting nutrient deficiencies
  • Supporting mitochondrial health with evidence-based supplementation
  • Investigating infectious triggers through appropriate laboratory diagnostics
  • Addressing gut health to reduce systemic inflammation
  • Working with a qualified functional medicine practitioner to tailor interventions

Important: Recovery from CFS requires patience, persistence, and a comprehensive approach. There are no quick fixes, but with the right support and interventions, significant improvements in energy, cognitive function, and quality of life are possible.
