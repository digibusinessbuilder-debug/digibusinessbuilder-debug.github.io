GUT & MICROBIOME

Fibromyalgia and the Gut-Brain Axis: Can the Microbiome Influence Chronic Pain?

Jordan Casey, MS-CNFM · Master of Science in Clinical Nutrition and Functional Medicine, University of Western States

Medically reviewed by Fawad Ahmed, MD on February 25, 2026

Published February 25, 2026 · Updated October 15, 2026

Fibromyalgia may involve far more than pain alone — emerging research points to a complex interplay between gut microbiome balance, immune signaling, and nervous system dysregulation.

Book a Consultation

Fibromyalgia is often described as a chronic pain disorder, but emerging research suggests it may be far more complex than pain alone. Some of the most common questions patients have are, "What causes fibromyalgia?" "Is fibromyalgia an autoimmune disease?" And "Can gut health affect fibromyalgia symptoms?" Yet finding clear, concise answers to these important questions can be challenging.

Patients are often left confused, relying on medications to manage symptoms rather than finding the root cause of fibromyalgia and addressing it holistically.

Fibromyalgia is more than just idiopathic pain. It involves a complex interplay between immune signaling, nervous system dysregulation, and gut-brain communication rather than isolated musculoskeletal dysfunctions.

From a microbiome-centered perspective, fibromyalgia may represent a multisystem imbalance in which the gut, immune system, and central nervous system interact in ways that amplify pain perception and fatigue. Understanding this connection provides a more comprehensive framework for addressing potential contributors to symptom burden.

What does fibromyalgia feel like in the body?

Fibromyalgia is characterized by widespread musculoskeletal pain, persistent fatigue, cognitive dysfunction ("brain fog"), and sleep disruption. It is estimated to affect approximately 2–4% of the population. It also impacts women at a much higher rate than men, suggesting potential hormonal or genetic influences.

Pain: why is it widespread?

The hallmark feature is chronic, diffuse pain affecting multiple body regions. Rather than tissue injury driving the discomfort, many researchers now describe fibromyalgia as a disorder of pain processing, meaning the nervous system is responsible for the pain rather than inflammation or injury in the area experiencing the pain.

Fatigue: why am I so tired?

Profound, non-restorative fatigue frequently accompanies the pain of fibromyalgia, often persisting despite adequate sleep duration. One key factor is altered central nervous system processing.

Fibromyalgia is associated with central sensitization, a state in which the brain and spinal cord amplify sensory signals. This same heightened neural activity that intensifies pain perception can also increase the overall energy output of the nerves involved, which contributes to exhaustion.

Brain fog: what is "fibro brain"?

Cognitive symptoms of fibromyalgia include difficulty concentrating, memory lapses, and slowed processing speed. Subtle elevations in pro-inflammatory compounds have been observed in individuals with fibromyalgia.

These chemicals influence both pain signaling and fatigue perception. Inflammatory cytokine activity is well known to produce "sickness behavior," characterized by lethargy and low energy.

Sleep: why is it affected?

Even when individuals spend adequate time in bed, sleep architecture may be altered. Research has shown abnormalities in deep (slow-wave) sleep in fibromyalgia, which may reduce the body's ability to repair tissue and regulate pain-processing pathways overnight. The result is waking unrefreshed despite sufficient hours of sleep.

What is central sensitization?

Central sensitization refers to heightened sensitivity within the central nervous system. In fibromyalgia, pain-processing pathways appear amplified, meaning normal sensory signals may be interpreted as painful.

From a functional perspective, supporting nervous system regulation, sleep quality, immune balance, and gut health may offer a more comprehensive framework for addressing this nervous system dysfunction associated with fibromyalgia.

The gut microbiome's role in chronic pain and inflammation

The gut microbiome consists of trillions of microorganisms that influence digestion, immune regulation, neurotransmitter production, and inflammatory signaling. Increasingly, researchers are examining how alterations in microbial composition may contribute to systemic symptoms, including chronic pain.

Dysbiosis and microbial diversity

Dysbiosis refers to an imbalance in gut microbial communities. A landmark study demonstrated that individuals with fibromyalgia exhibit distinct alterations in gut microbiota composition compared to healthy controls. The presence of specific bacterial species was correlated with pain severity and fatigue levels.

Intestinal Permeability ("Leaky Gut") in Fibromyalgia

Increased intestinal permeability allows bacterial components, such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS), to enter the circulation. This can activate immune pathways and promote low-grade inflammation, potentially influencing central pain processing.

While intestinal permeability has not been definitively established as causal in fibromyalgia, it represents a plausible mechanistic link between gut imbalance and systemic immune activation as a contributing factor to symptoms.

Immune activation and cytokine signaling

Patients with fibromyalgia often demonstrate subtle immune irregularities. Cytokines such as interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor-alpha have been investigated as contributors to symptom severity. Microbial metabolites and immune activation within the gut have been linked to influencing these inflammatory pathways in various gastrointestinal conditions.

Short-chain fatty acids

Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), including butyrate, are produced by microbial fermentation of dietary fiber. SCFAs help regulate intestinal barrier integrity and immune balance. Altered microbial diversity may reduce SCFA production, potentially influencing inflammatory tone.

Neurotransmitters: serotonin and GABA

The gut microbiota contributes to neurotransmitter production and regulation. Approximately 90% of serotonin is produced in the gut, and certain microbes influence gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) signaling. Because fibromyalgia involves altered pain perception and stress reactivity, microbiome-driven changes in neurotransmitter signaling are also clinically relevant when gut imbalances are suspected.

Collectively, these pathways form the gut-brain axis — a bidirectional communication network linking intestinal microbes, immune mediators, and the central nervous system.

What does the research show?

Research examining the microbiome in fibromyalgia remains early but promising.

A 2019 study identified significant differences in 19 bacterial species in fibromyalgia patients compared to controls. Notably, microbial patterns correlated with symptom severity, suggesting a measurable relationship between gut composition and pain burden.

More recently, research demonstrated that transplanting microbiota from fibromyalgia patients into germ-free mice induced pain-related behaviors in the animals, supporting a potential mechanistic role for microbial factors.

Metabolite differences have also been observed, including changes in short-chain fatty acids and other microbial byproducts that may influence immune signaling and neural activity.

The tone of current evidence remains cautious: microbiome alterations are associated with fibromyalgia, but the directionality and therapeutic implications require further investigation. As such, a functional approach that addresses all root-cause contributors, such as neural tone, sleep, inflammation, and gut health, is necessary for comprehensive care.

Why is fibromyalgia so hard to treat?

Conventional care for fibromyalgia often focuses primarily on symptom management. Medications may target pain perception or mood symptoms, yet root-cause exploration is frequently limited.

Managing symptoms vs. looking at the whole system

Pain-modulating medications may reduce symptom intensity for some individuals but may not address upstream contributors such as immune activation, sleep disruptions, or gut imbalance.

Overlooking gut health

Traditional approaches rarely evaluate microbiome composition, intestinal barrier integrity, or microbial metabolites in fibromyalgia care. If these components are a significant contributor to systemic inflammation, leaving them unchecked misses a vital root cause.

Overlooking nervous system regulation

Because fibromyalgia involves central sensitization, nervous system hyper-reactivity is often central to symptom persistence. However, interventions targeting autonomic regulation are inconsistently integrated into conventional care plans.

A functional medicine framework for fibromyalgia

Avicenna Functional Medicine approaches fibromyalgia through a systems-based lens, recognizing the interplay between gut, immune, and nervous system function:

Comprehensive Stool Analysis

Advanced stool testing may evaluate microbial diversity, inflammatory markers, and metabolite production. While not diagnostic for fibromyalgia, this information can offer insight into potential contributors to systemic imbalance.

Inflammatory Marker Evaluation

Assessing systemic inflammatory markers can help identify patterns of immune activation.

Nutrient Assessment

Micronutrient status influences mitochondrial energy production and immune function. Evaluating nutrient sufficiency supports a foundational approach.

Nervous System Regulation Strategies

Because central sensitization and hypervigilance amplify pain perception, strategies that support autonomic balance may be prioritized. This may include breathwork, stress physiology support, and sleep optimization.

Food-First Anti-Inflammatory Approach

Dietary patterns emphasizing fiber, phytonutrients, and microbiome diversity may support short-chain fatty acid production and immune modulation.

The nervous system, hypervigilance and pain amplification

Central sensitization means the nervous system becomes primed to detect threat signals in fibromyalgia.

The microbiome interacts with stress physiology through vagal nerve signaling, immune mediators, and microbial metabolites. Chronic stress may alter gut permeability and microbial composition, while dysbiosis may reinforce stress reactivity, creating a bidirectional feedback loop.

Understanding this interplay reframes fibromyalgia not as a mysterious condition, but as a systems-level dysregulation involving gut, immune, and neural communication.

What this means for patients

Fibromyalgia is complex, but complexity does not imply randomness. Research increasingly supports that immune signaling, microbial balance, and nervous system regulation interact in meaningful ways.

If you've ever wondered, "Can gut health improve fibromyalgia?" the evidence suggests that microbiome balance may influence inflammatory and neural pathways that contribute to symptom expression. While no single intervention fits all, systems-based exploration may provide new insights into tailoring an intervention plan to your unique situation.
