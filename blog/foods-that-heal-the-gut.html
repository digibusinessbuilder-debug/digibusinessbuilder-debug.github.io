Foods for Gut Health: Functional Nutrition for the Microbiome

Jordan Casey, MS-CNFM · Master of Science in Clinical Nutrition and Functional Medicine, University of Western States

Medically reviewed by Fawad Ahmed, MD on January 13, 2026

Published January 13, 2026 · Updated October 15, 2026

Supporting gut health isn't about one magic food — it's the combination of nourishing proteins, fermented foods, targeted fibers, and polyphenol-rich plants that support the gut lining and help shift the microbiome in a healthier direction.

Book a Consultation

The question of "what foods heal the gut" is a great starting point for supporting gut health. But what this question really asks on a deeper level is: What helps my gut lining repair, lowers inflammation, supports regular digestion, and feeds the beneficial microbes that protect me?

"The gut" is not just one thing; it's a complex ecosystem of various factors working together as a whole, each with its own specific needs and functions. The good news is that everyday foods can support each one of these gut-centered elements — when you choose them with intention.

The most reliable way to improve gut health overall is to combine soothing, nutrient-dense proteins, microbe-supporting fermented foods, targeted fibers and mucilages, and polyphenol-rich plants and spices that support gut-lining repair, promote healthy elimination, and shift the microbiome in a healthier direction.

Below is a practical food-first, gut-supportive roadmap you can start using immediately.

Bone broth, protein and collagen-rich foods

Bone broth is popular for a reason: it's easy to digest, comforting, and naturally rich in amino acids that your body uses to maintain connective tissue.

While "bone broth heals leaky gut" is often stated too confidently online, there is a reasonable functional nutrition logic here: the intestinal barrier is composed of rapidly renewing cells and protective mucus, and it requires a steady supply of protein-building blocks, minerals, and fluids to function well.

Why protein and collagen matter

  • Protein is our key "building block" nutrient
  • Cells that make up the gut lining turn over about every 3 days
  • The gut itself uses 40-50% of the protein we eat just in self-repair and gut peptide production
  • The gut tissue is 30% collagen

Think of bone broth as a supportive, foundational food, not a single magic fix. It's especially helpful when you're not tolerating heavy meals well, you're rebuilding after a period of digestive distress, or you need a gentle way to increase protein and minerals without a lot of bulk. Use it as a base for soups and stews, cook grains in it, or sip it between meals if that feels good.

If you don't tolerate bone broth (some people don't, especially those with histamine intolerance), you can get similar "collagen-style" benefits by emphasizing slow-cooked meats, bone-in cuts, gelatin, and adequate total protein.

Bone-in and skin-on meats

From a gut-health perspective, bone-in and skin-on meats matter less because they're "special," and more because they often represent a less-processed, more nutrient-dense way of eating protein. Skin-on poultry, ribs, shanks, oxtail, and slow-cooked roasts tend to provide a wider variety of amino acids than ultra-lean, muscle-only cuts. They also come with healthy fats when wild, free-range, or grass-fed meats are used, which is important for inflammation.

If your digestion is sensitive, preparation matters: slow cooking, braising, and stewing generally produce a softer texture that's easier to digest. Pair these proteins with soft-cooked vegetables and well-chosen fibers to help the microbiome thrive.

Fermented foods

Fermented foods are the best direct way to influence your gut ecosystem. In human research, diets higher in fermented foods have been shown to increase microbiome diversity more effectively than probiotic supplements and are associated with favorable immune changes, including reductions in certain inflammatory markers.

That matters because a diverse microbiome is more health-promoting and resilient — better able to handle stress, diet changes, and environmental exposures.

Examples

  • Plain yogurt or kefir (unsweetened)
  • Sauerkraut, kimchi, and lacto-fermented vegetables
  • Miso, natto, tofu, and tempeh (organic)
  • Vinegars, fish sauce, soy sauce, tamari
  • Kombucha (watch added sugar)

A key clinical note: fermented foods are not "more is always better." If you're histamine-sensitive, prone to flushing, headaches, hives, or you have significant SIBO symptoms, you may do better starting with small amounts (like 1–2 tablespoons of fermented vegetables or a few ounces of kefir) and observing your response, or starting with a low-histamine probiotic. The goal is gradual improvements in tolerance and consistency, not forcing it.

Fiber that feeds the microbiome

Fiber is food for the microbiota in your gut, and helps beneficial strains thrive. Dietary fiber shapes microbiome composition and function, in large part because microbes ferment certain fibers into short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) like butyrate, which support gut barrier integrity and immune balance.

Here are fibers worth knowing:

Resistant starch

Resistant starch behaves more like a prebiotic than a typical starch because it resists digestion in the small intestine and serves as fuel for colonic bacteria. Many people notice it promotes smoother bowel movements. Food sources: cooled potatoes, cooled rice, green bananas, plantains, lentils, legumes, apples, broccoli, oats, raspberries, spinach, artichokes, asparagus, onions, leeks, kiwi, jicama, sauerkraut, hard cheeses.

Beta-glucans

Beta-glucans are fibers found in oats and certain mushrooms. They can support regularity and are often well-tolerated compared with more aggressive prebiotics. Food sources: oats, oat bran, barley, rye, sorghum, shiitake mushrooms, reishi, maitake, seaweed, nutritional yeast.

Inulin and fructooligosaccharides (FOS)

These fibers strongly feed beneficial bacteria, but they can trigger gas/bloating in sensitive guts if introduced too quickly. Food sources: onions, garlic, leeks, asparagus, Jerusalem artichokes, chicory root, bananas.

Pectin

Pectin is found in many fruits and is often a great option when you need fiber that feels gentler. Food sources: apples, citrus fruits, pears, berries.

Psyllium

Psyllium isn't fermented as aggressively as some prebiotic fibers, which is one reason it's often tolerated well. It can support healthy stool formation, whether you tend towards constipation or loose stools. How to use: start with a small amount and increase slowly with adequate water.

The most important rule with fiber: increase gradually. A microbiome that hasn't been "fed" in a while can react negatively when you suddenly add a lot of fermentable fiber. Slow and steady is key.

Herbs, spices and polyphenol-rich foods

Many of the most gut-supportive foods aren't just "fiber foods" — they're polyphenol foods. Polyphenols are plant compounds that interact with gut microbes in a two-way relationship: microbes transform polyphenols into bioactive metabolites, and polyphenols help shape microbial balance and support the gut barrier.

Garlic

Garlic contains inulin-type fibers that feed beneficial bacteria, and it's been used traditionally to support microbial balance. If raw garlic is too intense, cooked garlic still offers value and is often easier to tolerate early on in a gut-support plan.

Ginger

Ginger is famous for nausea relief for good reason. In practice, it can be a helpful adjunct for people with sluggish digestion or post-meal heaviness. Try ginger tea, fresh grated ginger in meals, or adding it to smoothies if tolerated.

Turmeric

Turmeric's curcumin content is why it's studied so heavily. It's not a standalone gut treatment, but it's a strong supporting agent in anti-inflammatory pathways, especially when paired with a diverse, fiber-rich diet. (Tip: Pairing turmeric with black pepper and fat improves absorption for many people.)

Herbal allies

Depending on your needs and tolerances, herbs like peppermint (for cramping), fennel (for gas/bloating), and chamomile (for soothing) can be gentle additions. They won't replace foundational nutrition, but they can make the process more comfortable.

Berries and pomegranates

Berries and pomegranate stand out because they're rich in polyphenols that can support healthier microbial activity and gut barrier function. Use them daily if you can: blueberries in yogurt, raspberries on oatmeal, pomegranate arils in salads, or unsweetened pomegranate added to sparkling water.

Putting it together: a gut-friendly plate

A "gut-friendly" day of eating doesn't need to be complicated. Aim for:

  • A collagen-rich or well-cooked protein (bone-in/skin-on when possible)
  • One fermented food in a tolerated amount
  • One to two targeted fibers introduced gradually
  • Color from polyphenol-rich fruits, herbs, and spices

When you combine these consistently, you're not just chasing symptom relief; you're working toward a gut environment that's more stable, more diverse, and more resilient.
