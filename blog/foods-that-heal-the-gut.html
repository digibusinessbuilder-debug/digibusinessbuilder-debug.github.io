Foods That Support Gut Health: What the Research Says

Jordan Casey, MS-CNFM · Master of Science in Clinical Nutrition and Functional Medicine, University of Western States

Medically reviewed by Fawad Ahmed, MD on January 13, 2026

Published January 13, 2026 · Updated October 24, 2026

Editor's note: Revised by the Avicenna Functional Medicine team on October 24, 2026: every statement was checked against current research, and sources were added.

Supporting gut health isn't about one magic food. Here's what research shows about protein foods, fermented foods, fiber and polyphenol-rich plants, and how to put them together on your plate.

Book a Consultation

Many people want to know which foods are best for the gut. A more useful question is: which foods help keep digestion regular and feed the microbes that live in your gut?

Your gut is home to trillions of bacteria and other microbes, together called the microbiome. What you eat is one of the major things that shapes it.

The Dietary Guidelines for Americans say that vegetables, fruits, fermented foods and high-fiber foods support a diverse microbiome (a wide variety of gut microbes), which may be beneficial for health.

Below is a practical food-first, gut-supportive roadmap you can start using immediately.

Bone broth and protein foods

Bone broth contains amino acids (protein building blocks) such as glycine and proline, which your body can use to make collagen. But when researchers tested bone broths, the amounts of these amino acids varied a lot from recipe to recipe. They concluded that bone broth is unlikely to be a reliable source of them.

You may see claims online that bone broth is good for "leaky gut." But a 2024 medical review found that a direct link between diet and treatment of leaky gut syndrome has not been established. Your intestinal barrier includes a surface layer of mucus, a layer of cells, and immune defenses.

Why protein matters

  • Protein is a "building block" nutrient: you need it "to help your body repair cells and make new ones."
  • The cells lining your gut are replaced often: about every 3.48 days on average in people, based on a review of many studies

Think of bone broth as one food among many, not a magic fix. When you have diarrhea, broths are one of the liquids that can help replace lost fluids and electrolytes (minerals in your body fluids). Use it as a base for soups and stews, cook grains in it, or sip it between meals if that feels good.

If bone broth doesn't appeal to you or doesn't agree with you, that's fine. The Dietary Guidelines suggest a variety of protein foods, such as eggs, poultry, seafood and red meat, plus beans, peas, lentils, nuts, seeds and soy.

Choosing and cooking protein foods

Many whole foods contain healthy fats, including meats, poultry, eggs, seafood rich in omega-3s, nuts, seeds, olives and avocados. The Dietary Guidelines also say saturated fat should generally be no more than 10% of your daily calories.

If your digestion is sensitive, how you cook matters. The Dietary Guidelines suggest baking, broiling, roasting, stir-frying or grilling instead of deep-frying. If you have sudden (acute) diarrhea, it's best to avoid high-fat foods, such as fried foods, because they could make it worse. Pair protein foods with vegetables and other high-fiber foods, which support a diverse microbiome.

Fermented foods

Fermented foods are made by letting microbes, such as bacteria or yeasts, grow in a food and change it. Many, such as yogurt, kefir, miso, tempeh and unheated fermented vegetables, still contain live microbes when you eat them. In a small 2021 study at Stanford, healthy adults ate a diet high in fermented foods for 10 weeks. By the end, they had a greater variety of gut microbes and lower levels of 19 inflammation-related proteins in their blood. A high-fiber diet tested in the same study did not change the variety of gut microbes.

The Dietary Guidelines say a diverse microbiome may be beneficial for health. Still, experts have not agreed on what counts as an unhealthy microbiome ("dysbiosis"). More research is needed on how to use measures of microbe variety in medical care.

Examples

  • Plain yogurt or kefir (unsweetened)
  • Sauerkraut, kimchi and other fermented vegetables (heat-treated or pasteurized ones no longer have live microbes, and vegetables pickled in vinegar are not fermented)
  • Miso, natto and tempeh
  • Soy sauce and vinegar (made by fermentation, but heat-treated or pasteurized versions have no live microbes; soy sauce is salty, so use it in moderation)
  • Kombucha (watch added sugar)

A key note: with fermented foods, more is not always better. During fermentation, some bacteria can make histamine in foods such as cheese, meats, vegetables and soybeans. People with histamine intolerance (trouble breaking down histamine from food) may react to these foods. Researchers are still working out how to define and diagnose this condition. In the Stanford study, people built up their fermented foods slowly over 4 weeks. The fermented-food group reported more bloating during that time, but by the end of the 10-week diet the increase was no longer significant. Start small, go at your own pace, and stop any food that makes you feel worse.

Fiber that feeds the microbiome

Fiber is food for the microbiota in your gut. Your body can't digest most fiber on its own. Instead, gut microbes break down (ferment) many types of fiber and make short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) such as butyrate. Butyrate is the main fuel for the cells lining the gut, and SCFAs affect the gut lining and immune function. Eating many different plant foods gives your microbes many different types of fiber, which supports a more diverse microbiome. Some research suggests SCFAs may help the gut barrier, but a 2024 review found that no study had yet tested fiber as a treatment for "leaky gut."

Here are fibers worth knowing:

Resistant starch

Resistant starch is starch that your small intestine can't digest. It passes to the colon, where gut bacteria ferment it. Food sources: cooked legumes such as beans and lentils, peas, starchy foods that are cooked and then cooled (such as potatoes), and green (unripe) bananas.

Beta-glucans

Beta-glucans are a type of fiber. Among grains, barley and oats have the most, with smaller amounts in rye and sorghum. Some mushrooms (such as shiitake and maitake), yeasts and seaweeds also contain beta-glucans. But beta-glucans from different sources are not all alike. The beta-glucans in oats and barley are soluble fibers that gut bacteria readily ferment. Food sources: oats, oat bran, barley, rye, sorghum, shiitake and maitake mushrooms, seaweed.

Inulin and fructooligosaccharides (FOS)

Inulin and FOS are fibers that gut bacteria ferment. In studies, inulin increased bifidobacteria, a group of gut bacteria. But too much fiber at once can cause gas. Many of these foods also contain FODMAPs, carbohydrates that are hard to digest. People with IBS are sometimes advised to limit them. Add them slowly. Food sources: onions, garlic, leeks, asparagus, artichokes, chicory root, bananas, wheat.

Pectin

Pectin is a soluble fiber found in fruits such as apples. Gut bacteria ferment it readily. Food sources: apples and other fruits.

Psyllium

Psyllium is a fiber that gut bacteria don't ferment. It's a bulk-forming laxative: it absorbs liquid in the intestines, swells and forms a bulky stool that is easy to pass. It's used for constipation, and doctors sometimes prescribe it for diarrhea. How to use: follow the package directions, and drink at least 8 ounces (240 milliliters) of liquid each time you take it. Don't take it for more than 1 week unless your doctor tells you to. Before starting, tell your doctor or pharmacist about any medicines and supplements you take.

The most important rule with fiber: increase gradually. Eating a lot of fiber in a short time can cause gas, bloating and cramps. This often goes away once your gut bacteria get used to it. Add fiber over a few weeks, and drink plenty of fluids to help fiber move through your gut. MedlinePlus recommends 21 to 38 grams of fiber a day for older children, teens and adults. On average, Americans eat about 16 grams.

Herbs, spices and polyphenol-rich foods

Many plant foods also contain polyphenols, natural plant compounds found in fruits, vegetables, grains, beans, tea and coffee. Only about 5–10% of the polyphenols you eat are absorbed in the small intestine; most (90–95%) travel on to the large intestine. There, gut microbes break them down into smaller compounds the body can use, and polyphenols in turn can change which microbes live there. Scientists think polyphenols may also affect the gut barrier, but how this works is not yet clear.

Garlic

Garlic contains inulin-type fiber that gut bacteria ferment. It also contains FODMAPs, which people with IBS are sometimes advised to limit. Garlic can cause gas and belly pain in some people. Garlic has a long history of use for digestive problems, but much of the research on garlic has looked at cholesterol.

Ginger

Ginger has been used in folk medicine since ancient times, including for indigestion and stomach discomfort. Research shows it may help with nausea and vomiting during pregnancy, though most studies tested supplements rather than food. Ginger can cause heartburn, stomach discomfort or diarrhea. Try ginger tea, fresh grated ginger in meals, or adding it to smoothies if tolerated.

Turmeric

Turmeric gets its yellow color from curcumin, a polyphenol that has been studied a lot. So far, experts don't know enough to say whether turmeric or curcumin helps with any health condition. Turmeric can cause stomach upset, acid reflux or diarrhea. Some curcumin supplements are made to be absorbed better, for example by adding piperine from black pepper. Liver damage has been reported in some people who took these products. Talk with your health care provider before taking turmeric or curcumin supplements.

Peppermint and chamomile

A small amount of research suggests that peppermint oil in enteric-coated capsules (a supplement) may improve IBS symptoms in adults, including belly pain. The American College of Gastroenterology recommends it for overall IBS symptoms. It can cause heartburn. Peppermint tea appears to be safe. Chamomile is likely safe in amounts found in tea, but studies haven't produced enough reliable evidence to rate how useful it is. People who are allergic to ragweed or daisies are more likely to react to chamomile. If you take any medicine, talk with your health care provider before using herbal products.

Berries and pomegranates

Berries and pomegranates contain polyphenols. Blueberries, strawberries and cherries contain anthocyanins. In a study of healthy volunteers, blueberries increased two groups of gut bacteria, bifidobacteria and lactic acid bacteria. Pomegranates contain ellagitannins, which gut microbes turn into compounds called urolithins. The Dietary Guidelines suggest 2 servings of fruit a day for a 2,000-calorie diet. Ideas: blueberries in yogurt, raspberries on oatmeal, pomegranate arils in salads, or 100% pomegranate juice diluted with sparkling water.

Putting it together: a gut-friendly plate

A "gut-friendly" day of eating doesn't need to be complicated. Aim for:

  • A protein food, such as eggs, poultry, seafood, meat, beans, lentils or soy
  • A fermented food, if it agrees with you
  • High-fiber foods, such as vegetables, fruits, beans and whole grains, added gradually
  • Color from a variety of fruits and vegetables, with herbs and spices for flavor

Variety matters too. In a large citizen-science study (the American Gut Project), eating more types of plants was linked with a greater variety of gut bacteria. People who ate more than 30 types of plants a week had more varied gut bacteria than people who ate fewer than 10.
