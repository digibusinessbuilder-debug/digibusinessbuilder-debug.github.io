GUT & MICROBIOME

The Gut–Skin Connection: How Your Microbiome Affects Acne, Eczema & Skin Aging

Jordan Casey, MS-CNFM · Master of Science in Clinical Nutrition and Functional Medicine, University of Western States

Medically reviewed by Fawad Ahmed, MD on January 24, 2026

Published January 24, 2026 · Updated October 15, 2026

Emerging research shows that skin health is deeply connected to the gut microbiome — learn how gut balance shapes acne, eczema, and skin aging from the inside out.

Book a Consultation

The health of your skin is often treated as a surface-level concern, addressed with topical creams, cleansers, and cosmetic treatments. However, emerging research shows that skin health is deeply connected to what is happening inside the body.

In clinical practice, we often find that the health and quality of the skin are a direct reflection of the health and quality of the gut, particularly the gut microbiome.

The gut–skin connection describes the complex communication network between the gastrointestinal tract, immune system, and skin. When the gut microbiome is balanced and diverse, the skin is better equipped to maintain hydration, regulate inflammation, and defend against environmental stressors. When gut health is compromised, skin conditions such as acne, eczema, rosacea, and premature aging are more likely to appear.

Understanding how gut and skin health are linked allows for a more comprehensive, root-cause approach to skin care — one that begins with the microbiome.

The gut–skin axis

Gut health and skin health

The gut–skin axis refers to the bidirectional relationship between the gut microbiome and the skin. The gastrointestinal tract houses trillions of microorganisms that play a central role in digestion, immune regulation, nutrient absorption, and inflammation regulation. When the gut microbiome is diverse and resilient, it helps regulate immune signaling and prevents excessive inflammatory responses that can manifest on the skin.

Disruptions to the gut microbiome diversity and balance (a condition known as dysbiosis) can lead to intestinal permeability, or leaky gut. This allows inflammatory compounds, bacterial metabolites, and endotoxins to enter circulation, triggering systemic inflammation that often manifests in the skin [1]. Chronic inflammation is a well-established contributor to many dermatological conditions, including acne, eczema, and accelerated skin aging.

How gut inflammation shows up on the skin

The skin is an immune organ that responds quickly to inflammatory signals originating in the gut. Like your gut, the skin is a protective barrier that protects your insides from the outside world. They share many similarities. When inflammatory molecules circulate in the bloodstream, they can impair the skin barrier, increase oil production, disrupt collagen synthesis, and alter the skin's microbial balance. Over time, this creates an environment that promotes breakouts, flare-ups, irritation, dryness, and sensitivity.

Acne

The microbiome and acne

Acne is often associated with hormones and bacteria on the skin, but growing research highlights the role of the gut microbiome in acne development. Individuals with acne frequently show reduced microbial diversity and increased gut inflammation compared to those without acne [2]. Dysbiosis may contribute to acne through several mechanisms, including increased systemic inflammation, altered insulin signaling, and impaired detoxification of metabolic waste products.

Insulin resistance and acne

The gut microbiome plays a critical role in glucose metabolism and insulin sensitivity. When gut health is compromised, blood sugar regulation can become impaired, leading to elevated insulin levels.

Elevated insulin stimulates excess sebum production and androgen activity, both of which contribute to acne formation. Supporting a healthy microbiome can help stabilize metabolic pathways that influence skin oil production and inflammation.

Gut-derived inflammation and acne

Inflammation originating in the gut can exacerbate acne by activating immune responses in the skin. Pro-inflammatory molecules called "cytokines" increase follicular inflammation and impair healing, making breakouts more persistent and severe.

Eczema

The gut microbiome and eczema

Eczema, also known as atopic dermatitis, is a chronic inflammatory skin condition strongly associated with immune dysfunction. Research suggests that early-life gut microbiome composition plays a significant role in eczema risk, with lower microbial diversity linked to increased incidence and severity [3].

The gut microbiome helps "train" the immune system to distinguish between harmless and harmful stimuli. When this training process is disrupted, immune responses may become exaggerated, leading to chronic inflammatory conditions like eczema.

Food sensitivities are a common trigger for eczema. Dysfunctional immune signalling triggers systemic reactions, and symptoms of food sensitivities are not always experienced in the gut but can manifest as eczema instead.

Leaky gut and eczema symptoms

Increased intestinal permeability allows immune-triggering compounds to enter circulation, heightening inflammatory responses in the skin. This can worsen itching, dryness, redness, and flare frequency. Supporting gut barrier integrity is, therefore, a critical strategy for addressing eczema from a root-cause perspective. A diverse gut microbiome is a key factor supporting gut barrier integrity.

Histamine regulation

The gut microbiome also influences histamine metabolism. Dysbiosis may impair histamine breakdown, contributing to itching, redness, and allergic-type skin reactions commonly seen in eczema.

Skin aging

Inflammation, collagen and oxidative stress

Skin aging is influenced not only by sun exposure and genetics, but also by chronic inflammation and oxidative stress — both of which are strongly regulated by the gut microbiome.

A healthy gut supports antioxidant production, nutrient absorption, and immune balance, all of which protect skin structure and function.

The microbiome and collagen

Collagen breakdown accelerates in inflammatory environments. Chronic low-grade inflammation originating in the gut increases the activity of enzymes that break down collagen and elastin, leading to fine lines, wrinkles, and loss of skin firmness. A balanced microbiome helps suppress unnecessary inflammatory signaling, supporting collagen integrity over time.

Nutrient absorption and skin vitality

Many skin-supportive nutrients, including zinc, vitamin A, vitamin C, and polyphenols, depend on proper gut function for absorption. When gut health is compromised, nutrient deficiencies may develop even with a nutrient-dense diet, contributing to dullness, dryness, and premature aging.

Supporting gut health for clearer skin

Addressing skin health through the gut involves focusing on microbial diversity, inflammation regulation, and gut barrier support. Diet, lifestyle, and targeted gut-support strategies work synergistically to support a healthier balance within the microbiome and, in turn, the skin.

Key foundational approaches include:

  • Supporting microbial diversity
  • Reducing inflammatory triggers
  • Nourishing the gut lining to improve communication between the gut and skin

When these systems are supported consistently, many individuals may notice improvements not only in digestion but also in skin clarity, texture, and resilience.

Why a gut-first approach matters

Treating skin conditions without addressing gut health often yields only temporary relief. The gut–skin connection highlights the importance of addressing internal imbalances that drive visible skin concerns.

By supporting gut health, you may be able to beneficially influence inflammation, immune signaling, and nutrient status at their source, supporting improvements in skin health over time.
