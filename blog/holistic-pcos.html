PCOS: Root Causes and Nutrition Strategies

Jordan Casey, MS-CNFM · Master of Science in Clinical Nutrition and Functional Medicine, University of Western States

Medically reviewed by Fawad Ahmed, MD on November 15, 2025

Published November 15, 2025 · Updated October 15, 2026

PCOS affects roughly 1 in 10 women and is the leading cause of infertility — functional medicine looks beyond symptom management to the root causes of hormonal and metabolic imbalance.

Book a Consultation

Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) affects roughly 1 in 10 women; it's the leading cause of infertility in women, and one of the most common hormonal and metabolic conditions today. While conventional medicine often focuses on symptom management through birth control or medications, functional medicine takes a different route: uncovering and addressing root causes to support the body's natural balance.

This approach combines nutrition, lifestyle, targeted supplementation, and lab-guided personalization — aiming to help women rebalance hormones, support ovulation, and improve metabolic health in sustainable ways.

What is PCOS?

PCOS is a complex condition involving hormonal disruption, insulin resistance, and sometimes inflammation or genetic factors. Common symptoms include irregular cycles, infertility, acne, excess hair growth, weight gain, and polycystic ovaries on ultrasound.

At its core, PCOS is a signaling problem: ovulation doesn't occur on time, which leads to downstream imbalances in insulin, cortisol, and androgens. Fortunately, these signals can often be recalibrated through root-cause strategies.

PCOS subtypes

Insulin-resistant PCOS

The most common subtype.

Inflammatory PCOS

Driven by chronic inflammation.

Adrenal PCOS

Linked to stress and cortisol.

Genetic or phenotypic PCOS

Variations in hormone receptor sensitivity.

Identifying which subtype is dominant helps target the right interventions, rather than applying a one-size-fits-all approach.

Why we take a functional medicine approach

PCOS isn't just a hormonal imbalance — it's multifactorial. Metabolic dysfunction, inflammation, gut health, stress regulation, and environmental exposures all play roles. Functional medicine addresses each of these factors systematically:

Insulin resistance

Drives excess androgens, disrupts ovulation, and contributes to weight gain.

Chronic inflammation

Worsens insulin signaling and damages ovarian tissue.

Gut imbalances

Trigger systemic inflammation and affect hormone metabolism.

Adrenal dysregulation

Can mimic classic PCOS even without high insulin.

Environmental toxins

Can act as hormone disruptors.

By addressing these drivers, many women may see meaningful improvements in PCOS symptoms.

Nutrition strategies

Nutrition is the foundation of PCOS care. A few simple but targeted dietary shifts may help improve insulin sensitivity, hormonal balance, and fertility.

1. Low-glycemic, anti-inflammatory foods

What's the single most significant dietary change you can make that offers the greatest metabolic impact? Eliminate sugar-sweetened beverages. Liquid sugar causes rapid spikes in blood sugar and insulin — a primary PCOS driver. Reducing processed carbs provides further benefit.

Using a Continuous Glucose Monitor (CGM) can reveal how your body responds to specific foods, including fruits, which vary person to person. Aim for colorful, fiber-rich fruits, non-starchy vegetables, healthy fats, lean proteins, and anti-inflammatory spices.

2. Protein first

Protein is the only macronutrient that builds and repairs tissue — carbs and fats are primarily fuel. High protein intake is also the most practical weight-management strategy: it's satiating, regulates blood sugar, and naturally displaces excess carb/fat calories. Target: ~1 g protein per pound of ideal body weight daily.

3. Carbohydrates in balance

The human body can handle roughly 130 g of carbs per day — less if sedentary. The average American consumes ~300 g daily, fueling insulin resistance. For women with PCOS, ≤100 g/day during rebalancing may help support improvement.

4. Meal timing and gentle fasting

  • Pair carbs with protein — no naked carbs
  • Start your day with a high-protein, low-carb breakfast
  • Eat protein first at meals to blunt glucose spikes
  • Evenly distribute carbs across the day
  • Use a 12-hour overnight fast (no food 3 hours before bed, 1 hour after waking); avoid fasts longer than 18 hours to protect lean tissue

Lifestyle strategies

Nutrition is critical — but lifestyle habits amplify the results.

Sleep and circadian rhythm

  • Sleeping in a dark, cool room
  • Taking a warm shower before bed to lower core temperature
  • Avoiding caffeine after 2pm and screens after sunset
  • Getting morning sunlight within an hour of waking

Stress regulation

Chronic stress fuels adrenal PCOS and insulin resistance. Techniques like resonance breathing, physiological sighs, box breathing, EFT/tapping, prayer, gratitude, meditation, and nature time activate the parasympathetic nervous system, lowering cortisol.

Movement and exercise

Any movement helps, but resistance training is most powerful. It allows muscles to draw glucose from the blood independent of insulin, improving metabolic health. Plus, lean muscle mass increases your basal metabolic rate. Aim for 2–4 resistance sessions per week, daily low-impact movement, and occasional short high-intensity bursts.

Detoxification support

Fat tissue stores toxins, and the liver and gut play key roles in clearing them — including estrogen. Fiber, cruciferous vegetables, hydration, regular bowel movements, and sweating (exercise or sauna) support healthy detox pathways.

Supplements to discuss with your clinician

Supplementation is personalized — but some nutrients consistently support hormone and metabolic balance:

  • Myo- & D-Chiro-inositol (40:1 ratio): May support insulin sensitivity, ovulation, and fertility.
  • Vitamin D + K2: May support hormone, immune and metabolic health.
  • Magnesium bisglycinate: Enhances insulin receptor function and cortisol regulation.
  • Omega-3s (krill oil): May help with inflammation and insulin sensitivity, and support liver health via choline.
  • NAC & berberine: May support insulin sensitivity, androgen levels and liver detoxification.

Always personalize supplementation with guidance from a qualified practitioner.

Lab testing for PCOS

When approaching PCOS from a functional medicine lens, lab testing is used to uncover the root causes driving hormonal and metabolic imbalances — not just to label a diagnosis. A strategic, targeted lab panel provides deep insight into insulin sensitivity, inflammation, hormonal signaling, and nutrient status.

A well-rounded baseline panel often includes:

  • Complete blood count (CBC) and comprehensive metabolic panel (CMP) to assess overall health, liver function, and nutrient balance.
  • Lipid panel to evaluate cardiometabolic risk, which is often elevated in PCOS.
  • Iron studies and ferritin to check for hidden deficiencies or excess iron that can influence energy and inflammation.
  • Fasting insulin and glucose to assess insulin resistance and metabolic health.
  • Hormone testing (such as free and total testosterone, TSH, LH, FSH, and sometimes estradiol or progesterone, depending on symptoms) to understand ovulatory function and androgen status.
  • Additional specialty labs, such as the DUTCH test or oral glucose tolerance testing (OGTT), may be layered in based on the individual's unique presentation and goals.

The goal isn't to run every test possible, but to use labs strategically — starting with foundational markers and expanding as needed. This allows for a targeted, cost-effective, and personalized treatment strategy.

What a PCOS care program includes

A practical functional medicine approach to PCOS combines education, personalization, and accountability. While protocols should always be tailored to the individual, a well-structured PCOS program generally involves:

Initial strategy and intake

Reviewing symptoms, health history, goals, and lab data to understand the whole picture.

Personalized nutrition and lifestyle plans

Evidence-based dietary strategies, meal plans, supplement protocols, and lifestyle interventions designed around the individual's metabolic and hormonal needs.

Step-by-step implementation

Interventions are introduced gradually to avoid overwhelm, ensuring long-term sustainability and higher compliance.

Follow-ups and adjustments

As the body responds and adapts, protocols are refined based on progress, symptom changes, and updated lab results.

Education

Clients are guided to understand their bodies, identify their personal triggers, and build the confidence to maintain improvements independently.

This approach goes beyond symptom management. Functional medicine helps women understand and identify their unique root causes, support hormonal balance, and work toward lasting hormonal health.
