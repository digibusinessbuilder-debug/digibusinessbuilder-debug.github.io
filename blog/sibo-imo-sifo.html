Understanding SIBO, IMO, and SIFO: The Hidden Causes of Digestive Distress

Medically reviewed by Fawad Ahmed, MD on January 10, 2025

Published January 10, 2025 · Updated October 15, 2026

Discover SIBO, IMO, and SIFO—the hidden causes behind chronic digestive symptoms—and learn about evidence-based testing and treatment from a functional medicine perspective.

Book a Consultation

Millions of people live with chronic digestive symptoms—persistent bloating, unpredictable bowel movements, abdominal discomfort, and fatigue that seems to have no clear explanation. It's all too common for patients to hear "your tests are normal" while being left in the dark about real symptoms that you know are all but "normal."

A very common complaint we hear in our clinic is:

"I wake up and my stomach is flat, but by the end of the day, I look 8 months pregnant."

It's not in your head. There are legitimate diagnoses for such conditions; unfortunately, they're often missed in conventional doctors' offices.

Conventional testing often misses three specific, diagnosable conditions that could be at the root of your digestive distress:

  • Small Intestinal Bacterial Overgrowth (SIBO)
  • Intestinal Methanogen Overgrowth (IMO)
  • Small Intestinal Fungal Overgrowth (SIFO)

What you're experiencing isn't just a cluster of vague symptoms likely caused by "something you ate that didn't agree with you." These are measurable conditions to which targeted treatments exist. The key is finding the right doctor who knows what to look for.

Small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO)

Small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO) is characterized by an excessive growth of bacteria in the small intestine. Under normal circumstances, this portion of the small intestine should contain relatively few bacteria due to the high acidity levels from stomach acid and from the peristaltic movement of food through the GI tract. When bacterial numbers exceed 1,000 organisms per milliliter, this is a typical presentation of SIBO.

How SIBO develops

The small intestine relies on several protective mechanisms to prevent bacterial overgrowth, including:

  • Gastric acid and bile
  • Peristalsis
  • Proteolytic digestive enzymes
  • Intact ileocecal valve
  • Secretory IgA

When these barriers fail, bacteria that usually reside in the large intestine migrate upward and proliferate where they don't belong.

SIBO symptoms

SIBO often causes abdominal pain, bloating, diarrhea, and may cause weight loss and malnutrition in some cases if not addressed. Research shows that SIBO is associated with a wide range of conditions, including:

Gastrointestinal disorders

IBS, IBD, and other digestive conditions

Autoimmune diseases

Various autoimmune conditions

Cardiovascular conditions

Heart and circulatory system issues

Metabolic diseases

Diabetes and metabolic disorders

Neurological conditions

Brain and nervous system disorders

Who's at risk

SIBO can result from structural abnormalities of the GI tract, diminished gastric motility of the migrating motor complex (MMC), and complications of abdominal surgery.

Certain gastrointestinal conditions can affect intestinal motility or create intestinal structural problems, including celiac disease, inflammatory bowel diseases, irritable bowel syndrome, and pancreatitis.

Additionally, the long-term use of PPIs also increases the risk of bacterial overgrowth due to the chronic suppression of stomach acid production, a condition known as hypochlorhydria. Hypochlorhydria can also occur as a result of H. pylori infections that reduce acid production.

Intestinal methanogen overgrowth (IMO)

Intestinal methanogen overgrowth (IMO), once thought to be methane-dominant SIBO, involves an overgrowth of archaea, particularly Methanobrevibacter smithii, which produce methane. Unlike bacteria, archaea belong to a distinct biological domain, leading to the differentiation of IMO as a separate condition.

What makes archaea different

Archaea are the only living organisms within the human gastrointestinal tract that can produce methane gas. This distinction is crucial because IMO can be present throughout the entire gut, whereas SIBO only occurs in the small intestine. Methane is produced as a result of hydrogen consumption by the archaea.

Archaea are a normal part of our commensal bacterial population; they only become a problem when their populations overgrow other commensal bacterial species.

IMO symptoms

IMO has been implicated in the pathophysiology of various diseases, including constipation. While both SIBO and IMO can cause belching, flatulence, stomach pain, heartburn, anxiety, fatigue, and gas, a key distinction is that IMO causes constipation, while SIBO usually causes diarrhea.

Research indicates that methane gas has been shown to slow intestinal transit by impairing gastric motility through the MMC/peristalsis mechanism, which may contribute to constipation and is strongly linked to IBS-C.

A positive methane breath test is indicative of methanogen overgrowth, which has been associated with constipation-predominant irritable bowel syndrome. IMO has also been implicated in B12 deficiency due to impaired uptake within the gut.

How IMO is recognized

The medical community now recognizes IMO as a distinct and diagnosable medical condition. There is now an established ICD-10 diagnosis code for IMO (K63.829), meaning that insurance may cover or partially cover the cost of testing and treatment options, depending on the plan and coverage.

Small intestinal fungal overgrowth (SIFO)

Small intestinal fungal overgrowth (SIFO) is defined by gastrointestinal symptoms caused by excessive numbers of fungal organisms in the small intestine. Upwards of 26% of unexplained GI symptoms is the result of SIFO.

The Candida connection

The primary fungi found in SIFO cases are Candida species. Candida is normally a commensal organism found in small amounts in your mouth, on your skin, and in your GI tract. Similar to SIBO and IBS, it becomes problematic when it overgrows.

SIFO symptoms

The most common symptoms of SIFO are:

Belching

Excessive burping and gas

Bloating

Abdominal distension and discomfort

Indigestion

Difficulty digesting food

Nausea

Feeling sick to the stomach

Diarrhea

Loose or watery stools

Gas

Excessive flatulence

Systemic symptoms are also very common presentations of SIFO, including recurrent yeast infections, brain fog, and oral thrush.

Risk factors for SIFO

Small intestinal dysmotility and the use of certain medications are significant contributors to the development of SIFO. Long-term use of proton pump inhibitors significantly increases the risk of bacterial and fungal infections in your gut. Additionally, corticosteroids, birth control, and immunosuppressants also increase the risk.

Lifestyle factors that increase the risk for SIFO include chronic stress, diets high in carbohydrates, and excessive alcohol consumption.

Testing for SIBO, IMO and SIFO

When to get tested

If you're experiencing chronic digestive symptoms such as unexplained bloating, irregular bowel movements, food sensitivities, brain fog, and fatigue that seems to arise after eating (even after a clean, healthy meal), SIBO, SIFO, or IMO may be the culprit.

Testing can provide definitive answers and explanations for the symptoms you've been struggling with and offer greater precision in a care plan for addressing such symptoms.

Breath testing for SIBO and IMO

The most common method for SIBO and IMO diagnosis is breath tests with glucose or lactulose. These at-home tests measure hydrogen and methane gases produced by bacterial and archaeal fermentation. The North American consensus defines a rise in hydrogen ≥ 20 parts per million from baseline within 90 minutes as positive for SIBO, and a methane level ≥ 10 ppm at any time as positive for IMO.

How to prepare for a breath test

Proper preparation is crucial for accurate results. Patients follow a specific diet for 1-2 days before testing, avoiding foods that could interfere with baseline readings. The test involves drinking a solution (usually lactulose) and providing breath samples over several hours.

Testing for SIFO

The "gold standard" test for SIFO is with a duodenal aspirate, performed during an upper endoscopy. This procedure involves taking a sample of small intestinal fluid and culturing it in a petri dish to observe whether Candida colonizes.

This is a rather intensive and invasive procedure, so many practitioners use clinical diagnosis based on symptoms and response to antifungal treatments. A qualified functional practitioner specializing in gut health will be able to determine if this is an ideal and safe approach for your particular condition.

Comprehensive stool testing

Advanced stool testing can also provide insights into the gut microbiome, inflammation markers, and digestive function. These tests are more comprehensive and helpful for understanding the broader picture of digestive health, beyond just bacterial overgrowth.

Why professional interpretation matters

Because symptoms of SIBO, SIFO, and IMO can be vague and non-specific, testing is necessary to receive an official diagnosis. It's essential to work with a qualified functional medicine practitioner, as breath test interpretation requires clinical expertise.

Baseline levels, symptom correlation, and medical history all contribute to an accurate diagnosis. This is why working with experienced functional medicine practitioners is crucial for precise testing, thorough interpretation, and developing a personalized care plan.

Treatment

Evidence-based treatment for SIBO

Antibiotics are the most common treatment for SIBO, while treatment with specific antibiotics results in decreased methane levels that correlate with improvements in constipation for IMO. For SIFO, antifungal medications may be prescribed; however, dietary approaches also play a crucial role and may be the ideal approach for many, given the additional risk factors associated with antibiotics.

Functional medicine takes a holistic approach to care. There are several clinically validated dietary strategies, as well as powerful antibacterial and antifungal herbs and plant extracts, that have shown therapeutic potential for SIBO, SIFO, and IMO. Some studies show that herbal therapies may be as effective as antibiotic therapy.

Several studies have found that a low-FODMAP diet is one of the most effective dietary strategies for SIBO.

Finding the underlying cause

Successful long-term treatment requires identifying and addressing underlying causes. It's not uncommon for symptoms to return after completing a low-FODMAP diet or discontinuing treatment if the overall gut environment has not been effectively addressed and a healthier microbiome balance has not been supported and maintained. This is especially true if you have an underlying condition that predisposes you to bacterial overgrowth.

It's essential to address other GI conditions such as leaky gut, promote long-term microbiome balance, support healthy stomach acid production, and maintain a diet rich in fresh, whole foods.

Finding the right support

Specialized functional medicine clinics offer comprehensive testing and treatment protocols for complex digestive conditions, including SIBO, SIFO, and IMO. Look for practitioners who understand the nuances of breath testing interpretation and can address the root causes that lead to recurrence of overgrowth.

What to look for in a provider

  • Experience with SIBO, IMO, and SIFO diagnosis and treatment
  • Access to proper breath testing and interpretation
  • Understanding of root cause analysis
  • Comprehensive approach including diet, lifestyle, and supplements
  • Follow-up protocols to prevent recurrence

The functional medicine approach

Functional medicine practitioners take a systems-based approach, looking at the interconnections between digestive health, immune function, and overall wellness. This comprehensive perspective, along with a personalized care plan, is key to working toward long-term success.

Every individual is unique. It's essential that your care plan is tailored to your unique needs, root causes, and preferences.

If you're experiencing persistent digestive symptoms that haven't responded to conventional treatments, consider that SIBO, IMO, or SIFO might be contributing factors. These conditions are diagnosable, treatable, and—most importantly—you don't have to continue suffering without answers.

Working with a healthcare provider experienced in these conditions can help you receive proper testing, identify the root cause, and develop a comprehensive treatment plan tailored to your specific situation. Remember, your symptoms are real, and there are evidence-based solutions available.
