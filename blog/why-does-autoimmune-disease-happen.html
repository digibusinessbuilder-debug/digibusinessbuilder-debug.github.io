Why Does Autoimmune Disease Happen?

Fawad Ahmed, MD · Co-Founder and Chief Medical Officer

Published September 27, 2026 · Updated October 15, 2026

Genetics may load the gun, but environment, metabolism, the microbiome and immune regulation can influence how autoimmune disease develops.

Book a Consultation

Autoimmune disease happens when the immune system — built to distinguish "self" from "threat" — loses that ability and begins attacking the body's own tissue. That much is well established. What's less well understood, even among patients who've lived with a diagnosis for years, is why that breakdown happens in the first place.

Five factors behind autoimmune disease

Genetics load the gun

Certain genes, particularly those involved in immune regulation (like the HLA gene family), are associated with a higher susceptibility to autoimmune conditions. Having one of these genetic variants doesn't guarantee disease — many people carry them and never develop symptoms. Genetics sets the susceptibility; it rarely pulls the trigger alone.

Environment pulls the trigger

Something in the environment usually has to interact with that genetic susceptibility for disease to actually emerge. Researchers have studied a range of potential triggers: viral infections, chronic stress, smoking, certain medications, and environmental toxin exposure among them. This is part of why two people with a similar genetic risk profile can have very different outcomes.

Metabolism and inflammation

Metabolic health and autoimmune activity are more connected than they're often treated. Insulin resistance, visceral fat, and chronic low-grade inflammation don't just raise cardiovascular risk — they interact with the same inflammatory pathways implicated in autoimmune flares.

The microbiome's role

A large share of immune tissue sits in and around the gut, and the microbiome plays a documented role in training and regulating immune tolerance. Disruption to that balance — through diet, infection, or certain medications — is an active area of research in autoimmune disease.

Immune regulation itself

Beyond any single trigger, the immune system relies on regulatory mechanisms — including regulatory T cells — whose job is specifically to prevent immune attacks on the body's own tissue. When these regulatory mechanisms don't function properly, autoimmune activity becomes more likely regardless of what triggered the initial immune response.

Why this way of thinking matters

None of these factors act alone, and no single test explains a diagnosis on its own. That's exactly why our approach starts with Discover — a full history across genetics, environment, and lifestyle — before moving to Investigate, where testing targets the specific factors most likely to be driving your particular case, rather than treating "autoimmune disease" as one uniform condition with one uniform cause.
