What Hashimoto's thyroiditis is

Your thyroid is a gland in the front of your neck that makes thyroid hormone. In Hashimoto's, the immune system makes antibodies that attack the thyroid. Over time, the gland can become damaged and may not make enough hormone. This is called hypothyroidism, or an underactive thyroid. Rarely, Hashimoto's causes an overactive thyroid instead.

Hashimoto's is also called chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis or autoimmune thyroiditis. It usually develops slowly, over many years. Some people have the antibodies for a long time before their hormone levels change.

It is 4 to 10 times more common in women than in men. It most often develops in women ages 30 to 50, but it can start at any age, including in childhood. It often runs in families. People with other autoimmune conditions, such as celiac disease, lupus, rheumatoid arthritis or type 1 diabetes, are also more likely to develop it. The exact cause is not known.

Common symptoms of Hashimoto's

Many people have no symptoms at first. Some people notice a goiter, which is an enlarged thyroid. A goiter can cause swelling, fullness or a feeling of pressure in the neck, and sometimes trouble swallowing. It is usually not painful.

When the thyroid does not make enough hormone, symptoms can include:

  • Tiredness (fatigue)
  • Weight gain
  • Feeling cold more easily than others
  • Constipation
  • Dry skin and thinning hair
  • Joint and muscle aches
  • Low mood or depression
  • Heavy or irregular periods, or trouble getting pregnant
  • A slow heart rate

How Hashimoto's is diagnosed and treated

Diagnosis starts with your medical history and a physical exam, including a check of your neck for a goiter. Blood tests measure thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), thyroid hormones such as T4, and thyroid antibodies. Most people with Hashimoto's have thyroid peroxidase (TPO) antibodies. A thyroid ultrasound is sometimes used as well.

Not everyone with Hashimoto's needs medicine right away. If your thyroid hormone levels are normal, your doctor may simply check them over time. If you develop hypothyroidism, the recommended treatment is levothyroxine, a man-made form of the thyroid hormone T4. Most people with hypothyroidism from Hashimoto's need treatment for life.

Your dose is adjusted based on blood tests, which are repeated after starting or changing a dose and then on a regular schedule. Too little medicine can leave you with symptoms. Too much can cause serious problems, such as an irregular heartbeat called atrial fibrillation, or bone thinning (osteoporosis). This is why regular follow-up with your endocrinologist (a doctor who specializes in glands and hormones) or the doctor who manages your thyroid matters.

How our care fits alongside your endocrinologist

Avicenna Functional Medicine does not replace your endocrinologist, your primary care doctor, or the thyroid medicine they prescribe. Our role is to add to your care, not to take its place.

In a functional medicine visit, we look at factors that may contribute, such as gut health, nutrition, environmental exposures, sleep, stress and immune regulation, alongside the care you already receive. Our aim is to understand the whole picture of your health and to support your overall wellbeing.

Please do not stop or change your thyroid medicine, or any other prescribed medication, without talking to the doctor who prescribed it. Some foods and supplements, such as multivitamins with iron or calcium, can affect how well levothyroxine is absorbed. High-iodine products, such as kelp and iodine supplements, may cause hypothyroidism or make it worse. Check with your doctor before starting a new supplement.

When to get urgent care

Contact your doctor if you have symptoms of an underactive thyroid. If you are already being treated, contact them promptly if you have chest pain or a fast heartbeat, if your symptoms get worse or do not improve, or if you notice new symptoms.

Rarely, very low thyroid hormone levels can lead to myxedema coma. This is a life-threatening emergency that needs hospital care. It can be set off by an infection, illness, cold exposure, certain medicines such as opioids, or missed thyroid medicine. Telehealth visits with our team are not for emergencies. Call 911 right away for warning signs such as:

  • Low body temperature
  • Slow breathing, trouble breathing or shortness of breath
  • Confusion, unusual mood or behavior, or being hard to wake
  • Chest pain or pressure that lasts two minutes or more
  • Fainting or passing out
  • Sudden dizziness, weakness or changes in vision
  • Thoughts of suicide or of harming someone else (you can also call or text 988)
