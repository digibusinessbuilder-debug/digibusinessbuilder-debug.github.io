Gut Barrier Testing and "Leaky Gut" Concerns

Your gut lining is a barrier, and "leaky gut" is a popular term for a gut lining that lets more pass through than usual. It is not an accepted medical diagnosis, and no test has been validated to diagnose it. At Avicenna Functional Medicine, any gut barrier testing is one piece of a larger picture, used alongside the care from your gastroenterologist, not in place of it.1, 2

What is a "leaky gut"?

The lining of your gut works as a barrier. It has a layer of mucus on top, a single layer of cells, and immune defenses below. Tight junctions, made of proteins, seal the spaces between the cells.1, 2

This barrier is not a solid wall. Everyone's gut lets some things through, because the tight junctions in a normal gut are selective. Water and nutrients such as sugars, amino acids and vitamins cross the lining. Normally, small particles cannot pass through the tight junctions. Germs must get past the mucus and the cells to reach the immune system below.1

"Leaky gut" is a simple term for intestinal permeability, or how easily things pass through the gut lining. After damage to the lining, such as from inflammation or some medicines, the tight junctions may open and let small particles through.1, 2

"Leaky gut syndrome" is widely discussed in popular health writing, but it is not currently accepted as a formal medical diagnosis. Many things can affect how permeable the gut is, including illness, antibiotics and other drugs, alcohol and physical activity. Permeability also shifts with diet, exercise and infection.1

Gut barrier problems are well documented in diseases that inflame or damage the gut, such as celiac disease and Crohn's disease. They are also seen with ulcers caused by nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). Endurance exercise and pregnancy can make the gut more permeable for a time.2

Symptoms people link to a "leaky gut"

Many symptoms are blamed on a leaky gut, such as bloating, belly pain, diarrhea, tiredness, headache and food sensitivities. These symptoms are common and overlap with many other conditions. For example, 18% of people worldwide have bloating at least once a week. So symptoms alone cannot tell a leaky gut apart from other problems.1

There is currently no evidence that these symptoms are directly linked to a leakier gut. There is also little to no reliable evidence that a leaky gut causes conditions often blamed on it, such as fibromyalgia, chronic fatigue syndrome, allergies, headache and brain fog.1

Research has found changes in the gut barrier in a limited number of conditions, including:1

  • Celiac disease2
  • Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), such as Crohn's disease1, 2
  • Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), though the change is smaller than in IBD or celiac disease1, 2
  • Gut infections2
  • Type 1 diabetes1
  • HIV/AIDS1, 2

What the test looks at

Gut barrier tests try to measure intestinal permeability. Most measure it indirectly, through your urine, blood or stool. The main types are:1, 2

  • Sugar and urine tests: you drink a solution of special sugars, such as lactulose and mannitol. A lab then measures how much passed through the gut lining into your urine.2
  • Blood tests: some panels sold for leaky gut measure zonulin, a protein used as a marker of the gut barrier. They may also measure antibodies to zonulin, actin and lipopolysaccharides (substances made by bacteria).1, 2, 3
  • Stool tests: these may report zonulin, secretory IgA (an antibody released in the gut), bacteria imbalances and other markers.1, 2
  • Tests during endoscopy: an endoscope is a flexible tube with a camera. Special tools used with it can show dye leaking through the gut lining. These tools are still being studied.1, 2, 4

What the research says and doesn't say yet

Experts agree the gut barrier matters. But many questions are still open, and there are no validated clinical tests for it. A 2024 review in a gastroenterology journal concluded that no validated tool exists to accurately diagnose leaky gut syndrome.1, 2

Sugar and urine tests are the most common way to measure gut permeability in people. The lactulose-mannitol version is used as a gold standard, but it may not be as accurate as once thought. Their results are hard to rely on: normal values are uncertain, the steps are not standardized, and the tests have not been well validated.1, 2

Zonulin blood tests have their own problems: evidence suggests the commercial tests are flawed, and their results do not match disease states. A 2019 study found that two widely used commercial zonulin tests did not detect the actual zonulin protein. Its authors advised caution in using these tests to judge the gut barrier. There is currently no blood test that can diagnose leaky gut syndrome.1, 3

Stool tests sold for leaky gut are unregulated, and there is little evidence to support their use in medical care.1

Even if a test could measure gut permeability reliably, what the result means would be unclear. Diseases that inflame or ulcerate the gut do make it leakier, but making the barrier normal again does not, by itself, make any of these diseases go away. It is still unproven that improving the barrier eases symptoms. There is also no standard treatment for leaky gut syndrome.1, 2

Research continues. Special endoscopy tools have shown permeability changes in some people with IBS, though recent data suggest these tools may be less sensitive than first thought. Researchers see the gut barrier as a possible target for future treatments.1, 2

How our care fits alongside your gastroenterologist

Avicenna Functional Medicine does not replace your gastroenterologist or the treatment they prescribe. Our role is to add to your care, not to take its place.

In a functional medicine visit, we look at factors that may contribute, such as diet, alcohol, recent illness or infection, physical activity, and medicines such as antibiotics and NSAIDs, alongside the care you already receive. Our aim is to understand the whole picture of your health and to support your overall wellbeing.1, 2

If gut barrier testing is part of your plan, the result is one piece of information. It cannot diagnose leaky gut syndrome, and it is not a substitute for the standard tests doctors use for conditions such as celiac disease or IBD. Doctors typically diagnose celiac disease with blood tests and biopsies (small tissue samples) of the small intestine. A stool test called fecal calprotectin is used to assess IBD.1, 4

If you think gluten bothers you, talk with your doctor about celiac disease testing before you cut gluten out. Doctors don't recommend starting a gluten-free diet before testing, because it can affect the test results.4

Please do not stop or change any prescribed medication without talking to the doctor who prescribed it. Popular advice to cut out foods or take supplements to repair a leaky gut still needs confirmation, so check with your doctor before making big changes.2

When to get urgent care

Some gut symptoms can be signs of another health problem that needs standard testing. Contact your doctor soon if you have bleeding from your rectum, bloody stools or stools that are black and tarry, or weight loss.5

Telehealth visits with our team are not for emergencies. Call 911 right away if you have warning signs of a medical emergency, such as:

  • Chest pain or pressure that lasts two minutes or more6
  • Trouble breathing or shortness of breath6
  • Sudden confusion, unusual behavior or trouble waking up6
  • Fainting or passing out6
  • Sudden dizziness, weakness or changes in vision6
  • Being unable to speak6
  • Severe belly pain, or sudden severe pain anywhere in the body6
  • Coughing up or vomiting blood6
  • Thoughts of suicide or of harming someone else (you can also call or text 988)6
