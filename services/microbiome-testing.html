Microbiome Testing: What It Can and Can't Tell You

Microbiome testing looks at the bacteria and other microbes in a stool sample. The science is still new, and no microbiome test is approved in the US to diagnose disease, so these tests do not replace standard medical tests. At Avicenna Functional Medicine, any testing is chosen for you and reviewed alongside the care you get from your primary care doctor or gastroenterologist.2, 3

What is microbiome testing?

Your microbiome is the collection of microbes, such as bacteria, fungi and viruses, and their genes, that naturally live on and inside your body. Each part of the body, such as the gut, skin and mouth, has its own community of microbes.1

These microbes help protect you against germs, help your immune system develop, and help you digest food to make energy. Your core microbiome forms in the first years of life. It can change over time with diet, medicines and things in your environment.1

Your gut microbiome can't easily be checked while it is inside your body, but it can be studied from a stool sample. A microbiome test reads the genetic material (DNA) of the microbes in the sample to find out which ones are there.2, 4

Many companies offer gut microbiome tests that claim to help you learn about your health. But microbiome science is quite new. The field is only about 20 years old, and researchers are still learning a lot.2

Signs and symptoms that need a standard checkup

Many people who seek microbiome tests live with long-term gut problems. Many health problems can cause gut symptoms like those of irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), so doctors may order tests to rule them out. A microbiome test is not a substitute for that checkup, and relying on one could delay the care you need. Your doctor may suggest other tests if you have:2, 3, 5

  • Bleeding from your rectum5
  • Bloody stools, or stools that are black and tarry5
  • Weight loss5
  • Anemia, which a blood test can show5
  • A family history of celiac disease, colon cancer or inflammatory bowel disease5

What the test looks at

Many tests use a home kit. You collect a small stool sample in a tube with a preservative that protects the microbes' genetic material, then mail it to a lab.3, 4

The lab reads the microbes' DNA, usually with one of two methods: 16S sequencing or whole-genome (shotgun) sequencing. The report shows which microbes were found and how much of each there is, compared with the rest of the sample. Experts recommend showing these amounts as percentages, because they are relative, not exact counts.3, 4

Reports may also give diversity scores, which describe how many kinds of microbes are present and how evenly they are spread. Experts recommend comparing results with a matched healthy group. Some companies also label microbes as helpful or harmful, give a gut health score, or suggest diet changes or supplements.3, 4

Before testing, experts advise recording details that can affect the microbiome, such as age, diet, smoking, alcohol, other health conditions and medicines. They also advise not stopping your medicines or changing your usual diet before the test, unless your doctor recommends it.4

A microbiome test is different from the standard stool tests doctors use to check for blood or signs of infection or disease. Experts also say that targeted tests, such as PCR panels or bacterial cultures, can be useful but are not microbiome tests.4, 5

What the research says and doesn't say yet

Research has linked changes in the gut microbiome with a broad range of conditions, both in and outside the gut. But much of this data likely shows links rather than causes.3, 4

A 2025 consensus statement by 69 experts from 18 countries found there is not enough evidence to recommend routine microbiome testing in medical care. There are currently no regulatory-approved microbiome tests to diagnose disease in the US.3, 4

Gut microbiomes differ a lot from person to person, so scientists have not been able to pinpoint a "normal" gut microbiome. Experts say there is not enough information to set strict healthy ranges for each type of microbe. There is also not enough evidence to use "dysbiosis" (imbalance) scores in reports.2, 4

Results can also differ from lab to lab. In a study by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), researchers sent identical portions of one stool sample to seven test companies. The results were quite different. One company marked the sample as "unhealthy," while none of the others did. Three companies reported a disease-causing germ, C. difficile, and four did not. Overall, the differences between companies were as large as the natural differences between different people.2, 3

Experts recommend that microbiome tests be requested by licensed health professionals rather than ordered by patients on their own. They also say any change to your care based on a result should be made by your own doctor or health care professional. A NIST microbiologist advises against making major lifestyle or diet changes without talking to your doctor.2, 4

Work is under way to make these tests more reliable. NIST is working with researchers in the field to improve the measurements and confidence in the results.2

How our care fits alongside your primary care doctor or gastroenterologist

Avicenna Functional Medicine does not replace your primary care doctor or gastroenterologist, or the treatment they prescribe. Our role is to add to your care, not to take its place.

In a functional medicine visit, we look at factors that may contribute to changes in your gut microbiome, such as diet, medicines, smoking, alcohol, other health conditions and environmental exposures, alongside the care you already receive. Our aim is to understand the whole picture of your health and to support your overall wellbeing.1, 4

If microbiome testing is chosen for you, your clinician reviews the results together with your history, symptoms and any standard test results. Experts note that clinical decisions should weigh your whole history rather than a single test. A microbiome result is one piece of the picture, not a diagnosis.3, 4

Please do not stop or change any prescribed medication without talking to the doctor who prescribed it. This also applies before a microbiome test: experts advise keeping your usual medicines and diet unless your doctor recommends a change.4

When to get urgent care

Contact your doctor promptly if you have bleeding from your rectum, bloody or black, tarry stools, or weight loss. These can be signs of a health problem that needs standard testing, so don't wait for a microbiome test result.3, 5

Telehealth visits with our team are not for emergencies. Call 911 right away if you have warning signs of a medical emergency, such as:

  • Chest pain or pressure that lasts two minutes or more6
  • Trouble breathing or shortness of breath6
  • Sudden confusion, unusual behavior or trouble waking up6
  • Fainting or passing out6
  • Sudden dizziness, weakness or changes in vision6
  • Being unable to speak6
  • Severe belly pain, or sudden severe pain anywhere in the body6
  • Coughing up or vomiting blood6
  • Thoughts of suicide or of harming someone else (you can also call or text 988)6
